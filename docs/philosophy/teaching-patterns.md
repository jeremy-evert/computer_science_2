# Teaching patterns visible in the archive

This is an evidence-based snapshot of the archived course, not a new teaching manifesto.

## Practice before polish

The dominant later pattern is frequent, chapter-sized programming practice. Assignments commonly award partial credit for a serious attempt, then more for working solutions, and sometimes bonus credit for improvement, explanation, video evidence, or sharing code. The 2025–26 “three problems + mini-report” framing explicitly asks for code, run results, and a quick reflection.

## Open choice with a concrete demonstration

CYOAG and Coding Odyssey repeatedly invite a game, utility, simulation, or other student-chosen project while requiring broad use of course tools. Project rubrics emphasize an idea/design, progressively working code, and sometimes creativity, efficiency, clean code, or a presentation.

## Low-stakes reflection and human connection

Welcome/introduction activities, “show and tell,” career artifacts, and the repeated end-of-semester reflection make the course personal and vocational. The reflection language is unusually permissive: students choose five questions, any sincere answer earns credit, and a meme/quote or personal detail is welcomed.

## Collaboration and emerging AI practice

Fall 2024 and spring/fall 2025 contain repeated driver/navigator pair-programming assignments, explicitly allowing a peer or ChatGPT as learning partner and asking students to document the process. This is a significant late innovation; it should be retained as an experiment with clear attribution and learning-evidence expectations.

## Historical caution

Spring 2021 has a Java/Chapter-exam and discussion-heavy shape, while later semesters converge on Python and zyBooks-style labs. The archive supports a practice-heavy, supportive course, but does not preserve a consistent syllabus/rubric set across all terms.
