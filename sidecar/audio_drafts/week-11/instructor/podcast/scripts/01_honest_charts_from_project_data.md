# Honest Charts from Project Data

Computer Science Two Week 11, instructor listening edition. October 26, 2026.

**DANA:** Welcome to our Week Eleven instructor listening edition. I’m Dana. Today we’re getting ready to teach honest charts from project data. We’ll walk through the course micro-lab, connect it to the Odyssey gate, and talk about what useful student evidence sounds like. The aim is not to make every project look like a research laboratory. It is to help students ask a real question, use data their world actually has, and show why their chart is a fair answer.

**MARIA:** And I’m Maria. I’m glad we’re keeping the word honest in the title. A chart can be beautifully colored and still make a claim the data cannot support. Think of it like a map: a clear map helps you choose a road, but it cannot tell you what is waiting around a bend if nobody measured it. This week is about making a useful map and marking its edges.

**DANA:** The planning note gives us a compact sequence: start with a question about selected-world or project data, produce a readable labeled visualization, and defend the representation. Keep runnable evidence, then name a misleading alternative or visual pitfall. Plain Python collections or CSV and Matplotlib are enough. This is a way to strengthen the Odyssey, not a new plotting contest or a demand for a large data pipeline.

**MARIA:** That order matters. Ask first what decision or curiosity the chart should serve. Then inspect what information the project really keeps. Only after that should we choose bars, lines, or another representation. Otherwise students can fall in love with a chart type and go looking for a reason to use it. That is like buying a very fancy wrench before checking whether the problem has a bolt.

**DANA:** The instructor micro-lab gives us a small example from the Frontier Settlement world. Its question is: which settlement resource needs attention before the next supply decision? The provided CSV names four resources and gives one current recorded level for each. There are no dates, previous readings, consumption rates, or explanations of how the numbers came to be.

**MARIA:** So the data shape is four categories and four current amounts. It is not a time series. It does not say what caused a level, how quickly it changes, or whether any value is dangerous. The script reads the CSV with Python’s built-in CSV tools and keeps labels and integer amounts in ordinary lists. Matplotlib draws the chart when it is installed. If that library is unavailable, the activity has a small standard-library SVG fallback. The same reasoning still applies.

**DANA:** For a comparison among current amounts, the lab chooses a bar chart with the vertical scale starting at zero. The resource names label the categories, the axis says current recorded units, the chart title states the cautious observation, and the bars have values. Those labels let a reader check what is being compared without guessing. A clean chart is not just decoration; it carries enough context to be read accurately.

**MARIA:** The supported observation is that Water is the lowest currently recorded resource. That suggests a reasonable next step: inspect water consumption and replenishment before the next action. It does not prove that water is running out. It does not forecast a shortage. It does not prove why water is lower than the others. The line between observation and explanation is a boundary we want students to notice and say out loud.

**DANA:** Here is the pitfall the lesson highlights. A line chart would suggest a trend across time, but the file has no sequence of dates or repeated measurements. It would draw a story the data never recorded. Another pitfall is truncating the vertical scale. That can make a small difference look dramatic. Starting a bar chart at zero gives the bar lengths a fair baseline for comparing amounts.

**MARIA:** Students can also make a fair chart and still overstate its title. A title like “Water is running out” would imply a direction and a future condition. These readings cannot establish either. “Water has the lowest recorded resource level” stays close to the evidence. Encourage students to make the title answer the question, then ask whether every word is supported by the data they retained.

**DANA:** Let’s speak the micro-lab as a teaching walkthrough, without reading code aloud. First, name the question. Next, inspect the CSV and say what one row represents. Then read the labels and values into ordinary Python collections. Choose a bar chart because we are comparing categories, set the baseline to zero, label the axis, title the chart, and show the values. Save the output. If Matplotlib cannot be imported, use the provided SVG route instead.

**MARIA:** Then pause before interpreting. Point to the lowest bar and make the narrow claim: Water is lowest in this snapshot. Ask what action that observation supports. Maybe the settlement team checks the water status. Then ask what we still need to know. We would need repeated observations to talk about change, and other project evidence to investigate a cause. This is where the lab moves from representation to interpretation, limitation, and decision.

**DANA:** That sequence is the activity’s real lesson: question, available data, representation, interpretation, limitation, decision. The chart is one part of the evidence. Keeping the CSV and runnable script matters because another person should be able to reproduce the visual and examine what it used. A screenshot by itself can hide a missing step. Plotting syntax by itself does not demonstrate that the question, data, and conclusion fit together.

**MARIA:** The lesson also offers short practice repetitions rather than a second required homework sequence. Students might choose among a bar, line, or table for different questions and defend one choice. They might compare a zero-baseline chart with a truncated one, rewrite a title cautiously, or explain why an apparent association does not prove causation. Treat these as in-class, pair, low-stakes, or optional supports, just as the lesson frames them.

**DANA:** Now let’s connect the example to the Week Eleven Odyssey gate. Students submit a real question about data in their selected world or project, one or more readable labeled visualizations that answer it, and a reason the representation is defensible. They identify a misleading alternative or pitfall and retain runnable code and data, or a reproducible trace. The gate asks for a concise World Bible entry too: what changed, what evidence they used, and what debt remains.

**MARIA:** That World Bible piece keeps the chart inside the living project. It should tell another reader what the visual helped the student notice and how that evidence affected the world or the next decision. It does not need an invented grand narrative. If the student made no project change, they can record the evidence and remaining debt honestly. The gate calls this a small growth step, not a weekly mini-project.

**DANA:** The rubric scores twenty-five points. Eight are for a real question and labeled visualization. Seven are for reproducible evidence. Six cover representation and pitfall reasoning together with the World Bible. Four cover AI accountability when AI was used. That wording matters: AI accountability is conditional. We should not expect students who did not use AI to manufacture an AI story.

**MARIA:** The general capability evidence says the bounded change should fit the selected world, without rewarding an artificial hierarchy, recursion, queue, GUI, or algorithm. Evidence may be a test, trace, run, diff, or review receipt that supports the claim. Reasoning should explain the chosen boundary, abstraction, contract, event model, or workflow in plain language, as relevant to the actual work. For AI use, the student should show the proposal, diff, independent test, their own reading and reasoning, and an accept or reject decision.

**DANA:** For this specific gate, keep the assessment focused. A student needs a question grounded in real project or selected-world data, and a readable, labeled visual that answers it. They explain why the representation is defensible, identify a misleading alternative or pitfall, and keep runnable code and data or a reproducible trace. A polished picture without retained evidence is incomplete. A pile of plotting syntax without a supported question is incomplete too.

**MARIA:** Let’s make weak and strong work audible. Weak: “I made a chart because charts look professional. The line rises, so the problem is getting worse.” If the student has only one snapshot, the chart cannot show a rise. Another weak version is, “The graph proves the feature fixed it,” with no before-and-after evidence or other basis for cause. We can respond kindly: what rows support that sentence, and what would a reader infer from this chart type?

**DANA:** Stronger work sounds like this: “I asked which case category is taking the most attention in my project. My retained CSV has counts by category from this one review. I chose a labeled bar chart to compare categories. It does not show a trend or explain why the counts differ. A line chart would suggest an order I did not collect. Here is the script and data to reproduce it. I added a World Bible note about the follow-up question.” That answer can be much smaller than a dashboard and still show judgment.

**MARIA:** The example is a template for thinking, not a mandatory theme. A ship log, case queue, or small-business inventory may have different data and a different best display. Ask what each row means, which values are comparable, what order matters, whether time is genuinely present, and which labels would prevent confusion. If the selected world has no useful data for a proposed chart, help the student find a real question rather than inventing measurements to fill a slide.

**DANA:** Coaching can stay concrete. Ask the student to point to the exact data used. Have them read their title and axis labels as if the audience has never seen the project. Ask why a bar, line, table, or other representation serves that question. Then ask what a different choice might make the reader believe. Those questions reveal understanding without turning the conversation into a debate about favorite colors or chart libraries.

**MARIA:** The rubric is also careful about world fit. We should not reward students for adding complexity just because a chart can display more things. A small visualization that answers a meaningful question is enough. If a student’s world has naturally accumulated events or inventory records, help them use those. If the data is sparse, a table or modest chart may be the honest answer. The deliverable should fit the project rather than bend the project around a spectacle.

**DANA:** One useful distinction for feedback is observation versus interpretation versus decision. “This category has the largest recorded count” is an observation. “The workflow may be producing more cases of this type” is an interpretation that needs context. “I will inspect the intake step next” is a decision. Students can connect those steps while marking which one their data directly supports and which one needs more evidence.

**MARIA:** And keep reproducibility approachable. We are not asking for infrastructure. A reader should know what data file and script produced the figure, how to run it in the course environment, and what result to look for. If a dependency is needed, name it in the project’s usual way. The instructor example itself uses the standard library to read CSV and has a fallback if Matplotlib is missing. We can ask students to preserve enough evidence for a peer or instructor to retrace the claim.

**DANA:** For the AI portion, when applicable, ask for the same accountable chain used elsewhere in the course: what was proposed, what changed in the diff, what independent check ran, what the student read and reasoned through, and whether they accepted or rejected the proposal. The chart should remain the student’s defensible work. A generated chart or explanation does not replace checking the underlying data and the code that produced it.

**MARIA:** As we prepare to teach, let’s keep the Frontier example’s numbers in their proper role. They are the values in the supplied teaching CSV, not measurements from a student, a live settlement, or a forecast. We can mention the lowest recorded resource because that is what those four entries show. We should not make up dates, shortages, or causes to make the story more exciting.

**DANA:** Exactly. The excitement can come from the choice itself: what do we know, what do we not know, and what would we inspect next? That is a useful habit for any programmer working with project state. A chart is a lens, not a crystal ball. If we show students how to focus it and describe its limits, they can make more trustworthy decisions from their own worlds.

**MARIA:** So our teaching rhythm is simple. Invite a real question. Inspect the available data together. Defend a representation. Read the chart for one supported observation. Name a pitfall and a limitation. Preserve runnable evidence. Close with the World Bible note and, when relevant, the AI accountability evidence. The result is a compact, reviewable addition to the Odyssey rather than a detached graphics exercise.

**DANA:** That gives us a good place to begin Week Eleven. Ask students to bring one question their project can genuinely answer. Let the data set the scale of the claim. And if a chart starts telling a story that the rows cannot support, pause, redraw, or rewrite the claim. Thanks for listening. I’m Dana.
