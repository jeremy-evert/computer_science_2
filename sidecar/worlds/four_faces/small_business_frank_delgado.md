# Four Faces Dossier: Frank Delgado (Small Business)

Facet: **the operator**. See `README.md` for the shared performance
philosophy before using this dossier.

## Character dossier

1. **Name:** Frank Delgado.
2. **Title/role:** Owner/Operator, Delgado Hardware & Supply.
3. **World and institution:** Small Business -- Delgado Hardware &
   Supply.
4. **Why he has authority to contact the student:** It's his business;
   he hired the student directly to help get the shop's systems off
   paper.
5. **What he needs from the student's software work:** Tools that
   actually work at the counter and in the back office -- accurate
   inventory, honest numbers, nothing that requires him to trust a
   black box he can't explain to Yolanda.
6. **Core personality:** Warm, practical, proud of what he's built,
   allergic to jargon. Understands tradeoffs because he's lived inside
   them for twenty-two years.
7. **Competence:** Deep operational knowledge of running a real
   business -- inventory, suppliers, customers, cash flow. Not a
   programmer, and doesn't pretend to be; judges software by whether
   it actually helps him run the shop.
8. **Blind spot/flaw:** Occasionally undervalues his own operational
   knowledge as "just common sense" rather than the exact information
   a good system needs -- he'll mention something critical almost as
   an afterthought.
9. **What he respects in students:** Plain explanations. A student who
   can tell him what the system does in language he'd use with a new
   hire earns more trust than one who impresses him with jargon.
10. **What frustrates him:** Being talked down to, or a solution that
    requires him to change how the whole business runs just to fit the
    software.
11. **Speaking rhythm and vocabulary:** Plain, direct, a little
    weary-but-fond. Explains things the way a good boss explains a
    task to a new hire -- concretely, with the "why" included.
12. **Humor style:** Wry, self-aware -- jokes about wearing five job
    titles at once, never at anyone else's expense.
13. **Recurring prop/visual identifier:** A clipboard or order pad,
    held like he's about to check something off it.
14. **Wardrobe/presentation notes:** A shop apron or vest over a plain
    shirt, reading glasses. No costume beyond that.
15. **Signature document/artifact type:** The end-of-day tally sheet --
    plain numbers, no decoration.
16. **How he opens a briefing:** Starts with what's actually happening
    in the shop right now, not a greeting.
17. **How he asks for work:** Frames it around a real operational pain
    point ("I had a supplier ask if I knew what I had in stock, and I
    wasn't sure").
18. **How he reacts when something fails:** Practical, not panicked --
    "okay, so what do we actually do about it."
19. **How he reacts to a thoughtful tradeoff:** Genuine warmth: "That's
    exactly the kind of thing I needed someone to think about."
20. **Something he doesn't know yet (continuity):** He doesn't yet know
    that his own assumption -- that Delgado only sells single units --
    is going to break the first time a real bulk order comes in.

## Canonical entrance

- **Visual opening:** Standing at the back-office counter, order pad
  already in hand.
- **First line (normal):** "You're the one helping me get this off
  paper. Good."
- **Recurring sign-off:** "Let's keep the numbers honest." (used
  sparingly -- reserved for briefings that actually matter.)
- **Urgent variant:** No order-pad beat; already mid-sentence about the
  specific problem.
- **When pleased:** Genuine warmth: "That's exactly the kind of thing
  I needed someone to think about."
- **When skeptical:** A raised eyebrow, one direct question: "Walk me
  through that again -- in plain English."
- **When worried:** Quieter, more numbers-focused than usual -- worry
  makes him more concrete, not more anxious-sounding.
- **When annoyed:** Short, flat, a little tired-sounding. Never
  raises his voice; the tiredness itself signals it.

## Relationships

- **Who reports to him:** Yolanda and Denny; the student, informally,
  as the person building the shop's systems.
- **Who he reports to, if anyone:** No one -- he's the owner. His
  "authority above him" is the business's own survival, which is
  itself the pressure.
- **Who challenges him:** Yolanda, who's been there longest and isn't
  afraid to tell him when an old habit isn't working anymore.
- **Who he trusts:** Yolanda, completely -- twenty-two years of
  watching her catch problems before they became disasters.
- **Who he misunderstands:** Denny -- Frank reads Denny's mistakes as
  carelessness sometimes, when it's actually someone still learning a
  real job and trying too hard.
- **Who may later become important:** The new supplier whose data
  format doesn't match Delgado's system -- currently just a friction
  point, potentially a bigger relationship later.

## Continuity hooks

- A belief he currently holds that may be disproven: that Delgado only
  ever sells single units, never bulk/case quantities.
- A recurring operational problem: the gap between what Yolanda notices
  on the floor and what makes it into any system.
- A relationship under pressure: the new supplier relationship, if the
  format mismatch isn't resolved cleanly.
- Something he wants but can't directly order the student to provide:
  certainty that the business is actually sustainable at its new size
  -- the software can only show him the truth, not guarantee it's good
  news.

## Student relationship arc

- **Early semester:** Treats the student like a new hire being shown
  the ropes -- practical, patient, a little formal.
- **Middle semester:** Starts bringing the student real operational
  problems rather than clearly-scoped tasks.
- **Late semester:** Asks the student's opinion before making an
  operational call, not just a technical one.
- **Final contact/closure:** A plain, warm acknowledgment that the shop
  runs better than it did -- no big speech, consistent with his
  character.

Narrative trust growth must never be the actual grading signal --
students earn Frank's *narrative* trust by the same gate/checkpoint
evidence that earns their actual grade, not by roleplay participation.

## Performance card (quick reference)

**In one sentence:** A warm, practical shop owner who trusts plain
explanations and real operational thinking over jargon or cleverness.

**5-10 voice lines:**
1. "You're the one helping me get this off paper. Good."
2. "Walk me through that again -- in plain English."
3. "That's exactly the kind of thing I needed someone to think about."
4. "Okay. So what do we actually do about it."
5. "I trust my memory. I don't trust it at this size."
6. "I don't need it fancy. I need it right."
7. "Let's keep the numbers honest."
8. "Twenty-two years, I've earned the right to be tired. Doesn't mean
   I'm done."
9. "Ask Yolanda. She'll tell you what actually happens on the floor."
10. "Good system's the one I don't have to think about at 6am."

**Phrases/tendencies that fit:** plain concrete language, real
operational stakes, wry self-aware humor about being overworked.

**Phrases/behaviors that do not fit:** business-jargon fluency,
"disruption"/"scale"/startup-speak, treating employees as line items,
folksy caricature ("aw shucks" energy).

**Improvisation boundaries:** Stay plain and concrete. If in doubt,
explain it the way you'd explain it to a new hire on their first day.

## Sample briefing scripts

**Script 1 -- normal briefing (~30s)**

> *(At the back-office counter, order pad in hand.)* "You're the one
> helping me get this off paper. Good." *(sets pad down)* "Twenty-two
> years, I've run this place on that pad and what's in my head. Worked
> fine when it was just me and Yolanda. Doesn't work anymore -- I had a
> supplier ask me last week if I even knew what I had in stock, and I
> had to admit I wasn't sure."

**Script 2 -- urgent variant (~25s)**

> *(Already mid-motion, no pad beat.)* "Denny just told me the
> inventory count's off again, third time this month. I don't think
> it's him -- I think it's the system letting it happen. I need to
> know which, and I need to know today."
