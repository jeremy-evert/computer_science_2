# Prompt 017: Build the Four Living Worlds Bible

**Status:** OPEN  
**Course:** COMSC-1053 Computer Science II, Fall 2026  
**Owner:** Cleo  
**Scope:** Internal creative canon for the four Reasoning Odyssey worlds

## Mission

Build the **Four Living Worlds Bible** that sits behind the Reasoning Odyssey.

This is where the playground becomes real.

Prompt 015 builds the professional CS2 slide system. Prompt 016 explains the shared academic fabric of the Reasoning Odyssey. Prompt 017 now creates the internal canon that lets the four optional worlds develop personality, continuity, recurring characters, consequences, surprises, and long memory across the semester.

Do not make four reskinned assignment sheets.

Build four places worth returning to.

The academic contract remains shared across the course. The worlds provide context, flavor, continuity, and narrative energy around that contract.

## Governing architecture

Preserve this separation:

> Classroom materials teach the curriculum.  
> The Odyssey defines the work.  
> The worlds make students care what happens next.

Normal classroom slides must continue to work for everyone and must not require lore knowledge.

The four worlds are a separate creative layer that may support:

- briefing videos
- character performances
- scripts
- short transmissions
- memos
- artifacts
- reports
- mysteries
- emergencies
- recurring jokes that do not become prerequisites
- callbacks to earlier decisions
- world-specific examples
- optional narrative injections

A student who ignores the lore must still be able to succeed completely in the course.

The fun is an amplifier, not a toll booth.

## First task: reconcile the canonical four worlds

Before writing canon, inspect the current CS2 repository and any authoritative Reasoning Odyssey / World Bible source it references.

Jeremy and ChatGPT have been discussing four working world identities:

- Frontier Settlement
- Investigation Bureau
- Starship Log
- Small Business

Treat those as working labels, not permission to overwrite stronger existing source truth.

If the repository already defines different canonical names, premises, or boundaries, preserve the current authoritative material and document the reconciliation rather than silently replacing it.

Do not invent a fifth student world.

## Design target: four genuinely different living worlds

All four worlds must support the same CS2 learning outcomes and grading contract, but they should not feel like noun substitution.

Each world should have its own:

- central premise
- emotional texture
- stakes
- institutions or groups
- recurring characters
- places
- vocabulary
- kinds of problems
- visual language
- document/artifact style
- sense of humor
- sources of pressure and failure
- kinds of decisions that naturally matter

The same CS2 capability should be capable of surfacing differently in each world.

For example, a contract/interface problem in one world may arise from incompatible ship systems, while another world may surface it through business partners, agencies, equipment, or civic roles. Do not simply swap nouns around identical prose.

## Build internal bibles, not student encyclopedias

These bibles are primarily for Jeremy, Cleo, ChatGPT, and future agents creating world material.

Students should experience the worlds through carefully selected fragments over time.

Do not plan to hand them giant lore dumps.

For each world, create an internal bible that includes at minimum:

### 1. Core premise

What is this place?

Why does software naturally matter here?

What kind of role does the student inhabit without forcing theatrical participation?

### 2. Tone and flavor

Define what makes this world emotionally distinct.

Include both what it **is** and what it **must not become**.

Avoid cheap genre parody, stereotypes, or exhausting gimmicks.

### 3. Setting and boundaries

Define the key environment, institutions, locations, systems, and constraints that can repeatedly generate CS2 problems.

Leave enough negative space for improvisation later.

### 4. Recurring characters

Create a small cast with clear voices, motivations, relationships, responsibilities, and reasons to contact the student.

Characters should be useful for future briefings and injections, not merely decorative biographies.

Where appropriate, identify characters Jeremy could portray in short videos or live bits.

Give those performance roles enough specificity that Jeremy can have fun inhabiting them without needing theater-school preparation.

### 5. Jeremy's playable personas

Design one or more plausible briefing personas for Jeremy in each world.

These personas may eventually support physical props, clothing, badges, voices, or recurring presentation bits.

Current creative examples include ideas such as:

- a frontier figure with suspenders / old-settlement visual language
- a command or mission persona for the starship world
- an investigator / bureau leader
- an owner, operator, manager, or other practical business-world character

Treat these as sparks, not mandatory costumes.

The persona should serve the world and the learning moment rather than becoming the joke itself.

### 6. Visual identity

Define a restrained but unmistakable visual vocabulary for each world.

Include guidance for:

- typography or type mood
- imagery
- diagrams
- title cards
- briefing screens
- artifacts/documents
- iconography
- recurring visual motifs

Do not create four inaccessible or visually chaotic design systems.

World materials should still feel related to the larger CS2 presentation system.

### 7. Artifact language

Define the kinds of in-world objects we can hand students or show them later, such as:

- logs
- dispatches
- field reports
- customer records
- incident reports
- manifests
- diagrams
- maps
- requests
- maintenance notes
- meeting notes
- evidence packets
- status reports

Each world should have artifacts that feel native to it.

### 8. Natural CS2 problem generators

Map the world to the semester's actual technical progression.

Do not rewrite the curriculum.

Identify how the existing CS2 capabilities can naturally create problems in the world, including where appropriate:

- object boundaries
- composition
- invariants
- inheritance and polymorphism
- interfaces / contracts
- collections
- search and ordering
- GUI / model-view separation
- data visualization and storytelling
- peer review and stabilization
- source management / reproducibility
- later shared experiences such as Farkle / ML where relevant

The purpose is to help future prompts create authentic world-specific problems without building four different courses.

### 9. Continuity ledger

Create a lightweight mechanism for recording canonical events as the semester unfolds.

It should track things such as:

- what happened
- when it happened
- which characters were involved
- what students learned
- what software/world decision was affected
- what unresolved thread remains
- whether the event may safely return later

Future agents should be able to consult this before writing new material and avoid continuity errors.

### 10. Seeds and delayed payoffs

Plant several possible threads in each world that can begin quietly and return later.

Examples of structures, not mandatory stories:

- an early assumption that later becomes expensive
- a minor character who returns with a larger responsibility
- a system that initially works but fails under new constraints
- an unexplained discrepancy that eventually becomes a data problem
- a policy or rule whose unintended consequence appears weeks later
- a design compromise that must be defended or refactored

Do not force every seed to pay off.

Give us a garden, not railroad tracks.

### 11. Injection menu

For each world, propose a bank of short future narrative injections that could be deployed when pedagogically useful.

Include a mixture of:

- 30–60 second video briefings
- written messages
- one-slide interruptions
- artifacts
- urgent requests
- callbacks
- small mysteries
- character updates

Do not fully produce the semester's injections in this prompt.

Create the menu and enough prototypes to prove the world can speak.

### 12. Safety, inclusion, and taste boundaries

Document what future authors should avoid.

The worlds must not depend on:

- cultural caricature
- gender stereotypes
- race or nationality jokes
- disability jokes
- student humiliation
- political litmus tests
- violence as cheap spectacle
- inaccessible genre knowledge
- roleplay participation as a grading requirement

Students should be able to enjoy the flavor without being asked to perform a personality they do not want to perform.

## The worlds must have memory

This is the most important creative requirement.

The world should remember.

A Week 3 choice may be referenced in Week 8.

A character may remember what the student decided.

A World Bible entry may become relevant when a later requirement places pressure on the old design.

A previously harmless assumption may become the source of a later failure.

This continuity should reward attention without punishing a student who missed a joke or ignored optional lore.

Whenever a callback affects graded work, the necessary technical context must be restated clearly outside the lore.

## Relationship to the student's World Bible

Do not confuse the **internal Four Living Worlds Bible** with the student's **World Bible**.

The internal bible is our canon and authoring guide.

The student's World Bible records the student's own software world, assumptions, responsibilities, invariants, interfaces, design choices, revisions, and evidence.

The two may interact, but the internal canon must never overwrite student design agency.

World events should create pressure and choices, not secretly dictate one correct object model.

## Build for performance

Jeremy is willing to inhabit these worlds.

Use that.

Design recurring roles and briefing formats that could support memorable live or recorded moments with simple practical props, clothing, badges, documents, or set dressing.

But keep class.

The goal is not a costume gag.

The goal is the delightful moment when a student recognizes, before a word is spoken, **which world is calling**.

## Deliverables

Produce:

1. a master **Four Living Worlds Bible** explaining shared rules and authoring boundaries
2. one substantial internal world bible for each of the four canonical worlds
3. a recurring-character roster for each world
4. Jeremy persona / performance notes for each world
5. visual-language guidance for each world
6. native artifact/document patterns for each world
7. a CS2 capability-to-world opportunity map
8. a continuity-ledger format and initial seeded entries
9. several optional long-running story seeds per world
10. an injection menu for each world
11. at least one small prototype artifact or briefing concept per world proving that each world has a distinct voice
12. a concise guide for future agents: **how to write inside these worlds without breaking canon or the academic firewall**

## Acceptance criteria

Prompt 017 is complete when:

- the four worlds are genuinely distinguishable without relying only on different nouns
- each world has a coherent cast, tone, setting, problem ecology, and visual/artifact language
- each world can naturally generate problems aligned with the existing CS2 technical spine
- Jeremy has playable persona options for all four worlds
- future videos, scripts, slides, and injections can be authored consistently from the bibles
- continuity can be recorded and consulted
- the worlds contain seeds capable of delayed payoff
- no lore knowledge is required to understand normal class instruction
- no roleplay participation is required for academic success
- all four worlds remain academically equivalent
- the student's World Bible remains their design record and is not replaced by internal canon
- future agents can create world-specific material without inventing a second curriculum

## Explicitly out of scope

Do **not** in this prompt:

- rewrite the CS2 learning outcomes
- change grading
- build four separate syllabi
- build four separate weekly courses
- rewrite Prompt 016's shared Reasoning Odyssey orientation
- convert normal classroom decks into world-specific decks
- produce every future video or narrative injection
- force every seeded story to have a predetermined ending
- reveal the full internal canon to students

Build the backstage machinery.

Then let the worlds start knocking on the classroom door.
