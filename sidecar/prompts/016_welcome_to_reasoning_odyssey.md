# Prompt 016: Welcome to the Reasoning Odyssey

**Status:** OPEN  
**Course:** COMSC-1053 Computer Science II, Fall 2026  
**Owner:** Cleo  
**Scope:** Student-facing Reasoning Odyssey orientation deck only

## Mission

Using the accepted CS2 slide system from Prompt 015, build a fifth presentation that explains the **Reasoning Odyssey itself**.

This is not a normal weekly lecture deck and it is not one of the four world-lore presentations.

Its job is to explain the shared academic fabric that connects every student regardless of which world they choose:

- what the Reasoning Odyssey is
- what students are building across the semester
- what the World Bible is
- what evidence students produce
- how gates and checkpoints work
- how design decisions accumulate and can return later
- how students may choose among the available worlds
- how all worlds still satisfy the same CS2 learning outcomes and grading contract

The deck should give the course a real opening moment while remaining unmistakably appropriate for a university Computer Science II course.

## Architectural firewall

Maintain a strict separation between three layers.

### 1. Class slides

Normal CS2 teaching decks remain professional course materials.

They teach the shared curriculum across the full class and may draw examples from multiple domains. They must not require students to understand lore or roleplay in order to learn the material.

### 2. Reasoning Odyssey orientation

This Prompt 016 deck explains the shared structure, expectations, deliverables, choices, and purpose of the Odyssey.

It is the bridge between the academic course and the optional flavor of the four worlds.

### 3. The four world playgrounds

The four worlds are separate out-of-class narrative spaces.

Their lore may later contain:

- character briefings
- videos
- scripts
- world-specific slides
- transmissions
- memos
- artifacts
- mysteries
- emergencies
- recurring characters
- instructor roleplay
- narrative injections tied to student progress

Do **not** build those world experiences in this prompt.

The class should work perfectly for a student who enjoys the lore and for a student who ignores it completely.

The fun is an amplifier, not a toll booth.

## Core design principle

Use this as the governing rule:

> Classroom materials teach the curriculum.  
> The Odyssey defines the work.  
> The worlds make students care what happens next.

The deck should make that structure understandable without literally needing to show this exact wording to students unless it works naturally.

## Opening tone

Give students a genuine opening moment.

The tone should be confident, intriguing, and a little dramatic without becoming fantasy theater or cheesy lore.

A useful conceptual anchor is:

> You are going to build one software world all semester. It will start simple. Then we are going to stress it, break assumptions, add capabilities, and make you explain why it still deserves to exist.

Do not turn that sentence into empty spectacle. The deck must quickly connect the idea to real CS2 work.

## Explain the semester-long object

Students should understand that they are not completing a pile of unrelated coding exercises.

They are growing and revising a software world over time.

The deck should communicate that:

- early choices establish structure
- later capabilities put pressure on earlier designs
- assumptions may fail
- students will refactor
- students will collect evidence
- students will explain decisions
- earlier design choices may matter again later

This continuity is a feature of the course.

## Explain the World Bible

Introduce the **World Bible** as the durable record of the student's software world and reasoning.

It may capture things such as:

- world rules
- object responsibilities
- assumptions
- invariants
- naming decisions
- interfaces and contracts
- architecture choices
- revisions
- evidence
- why a decision changed

Avoid framing the World Bible as decorative lore or busywork.

Students should understand that it becomes the memory of their system and a record they can consult when later work exposes consequences of earlier choices.

## Explain gates and checkpoints

Use the current course source of truth to explain the difference between recurring Odyssey gates and major checkpoints.

Do not invent grading mechanics.

Students should understand:

- what kind of evidence a gate expects
- what a checkpoint asks them to demonstrate
- that reasoning and explanation matter alongside working code
- that the same academic standard applies across all available worlds

Keep the explanation concise enough to teach from.

## Introduce choice without spoiling the worlds

Present the available world choices clearly enough for students to choose, but do not dump the full lore onto them.

The orientation should feel more like opening four doors than reading four encyclopedias.

For each world, give students only enough information to understand its basic flavor, kinds of systems/problems, and why someone might enjoy building there.

Treat the repository's current world definitions and World Bible sources as authoritative. If names, themes, or details in the repo differ from assumptions in older notes, follow the current source of truth.

Do not invent a fifth student world.

## Preserve surprise

Do not explain how extensive the out-of-class world layer may become.

Students do not need a roadmap of future roleplay, videos, injections, characters, or surprises.

They need the academic contract and an appealing choice.

Leave room for the worlds to reveal themselves over time.

## Roleplay boundary

This deck may acknowledge that the worlds have personality, but Jeremy's character performances belong to later world-specific material.

Do not make the orientation depend on costume bits, character voices, or private jokes.

Later world prompts can fully own that space.

## Use the Prompt 015 slide system

Do not invent a second presentation style.

Use the accepted reusable CS2 slide architecture, including its standards for:

- typography
- accessibility
- code
- diagrams
- pacing
- presenter notes
- transitions
- Reasoning Odyssey visual language
- World Bible visual language

If Prompt 015 is not yet accepted, stop short of final visual production and prepare only the content/storyboard needed for this deck.

## Suggested narrative arc

Do not treat this as a mandatory slide count, but the deck should roughly accomplish this progression:

1. **Welcome to the Reasoning Odyssey**
2. CS2 is not a stack of disconnected assignments
3. You will grow one software world across the semester
4. Your choices create consequences
5. The World Bible remembers those choices
6. Gates produce evidence of progress
7. Checkpoints ask you to defend a larger capability
8. All students share the same academic destination
9. You get to choose the world in which you travel
10. Brief, spoiler-light introduction to the four choices
11. How to choose without overthinking it
12. What happens next

Use fewer or more slides as needed. Preserve pacing and room for Jeremy to teach rather than filling every surface with text.

## Student-choice guidance

Help students choose based on taste, not perceived difficulty.

The deck should make clear that:

- no world is the easy world
- no world is the advanced world
- all worlds target the same CS2 outcomes
- students are choosing context and flavor, not a different grading standard
- they should choose the environment they would most enjoy returning to repeatedly

## Deliverables

3. presenter notes where useful
4. a concise mapping from the deck to the current CS2 Odyssey deliverables and grading model
5. a short record of any source ambiguity discovered, especially around the four current world definitions

## Acceptance criteria

This prompt is complete when:

- the deck clearly explains the Reasoning Odyssey without teaching a normal technical week
- the World Bible has a concrete academic purpose
- gates and checkpoints are explained accurately from current course sources
- students understand that one evolving software world carries across the semester
- students can choose among the four worlds without needing hidden lore
- all four choices are presented as academically equivalent
- class-wide materials remain independent of world roleplay
- the world playgrounds remain available for later videos, scripts, characters, and narrative injections
- the deck uses the accepted Prompt 015 presentation system
- another agent could later build each world independently without changing this shared academic contract

## Explicitly out of scope

Do **not** in this prompt:

- build the four world bibles from scratch
- write full lore for any world
- create Jeremy's world characters
- script roleplay videos
- create world-specific injections
- build Week 1, Week 2, Week 3, or later weekly teaching decks
- alter CS2 grading or learning outcomes
- alter the Prompt 015 slide system unless a genuine defect is discovered and documented

This deck defines the fabric.

The four worlds get to become alive later.
